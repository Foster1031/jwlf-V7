The Power of Sponsorship for Corporate Women

January 18, 2025

When we talk about advancing women in the workplace, terms like “mentorship” and “sponsorship” often surface. While they may seem interchangeable at first glance, they represent fundamentally different dynamics — and one of them has the power to significantly move the needle for women’s progress in corporate environments. That’s sponsorship.

Mentorship helps women navigate the corporate maze. Sponsorship, however, doesn’t just give women the map — it helps them climb ladders, break glass ceilings, and open doors that might otherwise remain shut. Let’s dive into why sponsorship is crucial for women in the workplace and how it differs from mentorship in ways that matter.

Mentorship vs. Sponsorship: The Key Differences

Think of mentorship as guidance. A mentor is someone who provides advice, shares their own experiences, and offers a sounding board when you’re unsure of your next step. Mentorship is valuable, don’t get me wrong — it helps build confidence, provides clarity, and can equip women with essential skills.

Sponsorship, on the other hand, is all about action. A sponsor is someone who doesn’t just advise but actively advocates for you. They use their influence, network, and credibility to create opportunities for you. Sponsors will put their name — and sometimes even their reputation — on the line to ensure you’re seen, heard, and considered for key roles or projects.

  • Mentors talk to you. Sponsors talk about you.
  • Mentors guide you behind the scenes. Sponsors champion you in the spotlight.
  • Mentorship is about personal development. Sponsorship is about career advancement.

Why Sponsorship Matters More for Women

We all know that women have been underrepresented in leadership roles. Even as workplaces prioritize diversity, equity, and inclusion, systemic barriers persist. Here’s where sponsorship becomes a game-changer.

1. It Breaks Through the “Boys’ Club” Culture

Corporate environments often operate on networks — who you know and who knows you can sometimes matter more than your qualifications. Men have traditionally benefitted from informal sponsorship relationships within these networks. Women, particularly women of color, have historically been excluded from these spaces.

Sponsors can help level the playing field by ensuring women aren’t just part of the conversation — they’re leading it. A sponsor vouches for your capabilities, connects you to decision-makers, and ensures you’re considered for opportunities that might otherwise be offered to someone else.

2. It Challenges Implicit Bias

Research shows that women are often promoted based on performance, while men are promoted based on potential. How’s that for fairness? This bias means women have to prove themselves repeatedly before earning a shot at leadership roles. Sponsors challenge this norm by highlighting women’s potential and pushing for their advancement, even when others hesitate.

For instance, imagine a senior leader saying, “I think Sarah is ready to lead this project. Let’s give her a chance.” That’s sponsorship in action — someone actively advocating for Sarah and ensuring she’s considered for opportunities that stretch her abilities and demonstrate her worth.

3. It Closes the Pay and Position Gaps

Sponsorship can directly impact the pay and position disparities between men and women. By ensuring women are positioned for high-visibility roles, leadership opportunities, and promotions, sponsors help close the gaps that often widen as careers progress. A sponsor’s advocacy ensures women aren’t overlooked for executive roles, board seats, or critical assignments.

The Human Side of Sponsorship: Real Stories

Scenario 1: The Mentor. Emma is a marketing manager looking to grow into a leadership role. Her mentor, Lisa, is a VP of Marketing who meets with Emma monthly, offering advice on navigating office politics and honing her skills. Lisa encourages Emma to build her network and keep her resume polished. Emma feels supported but finds herself struggling to get noticed by senior leadership.

Scenario 2: The Sponsor. Now, let’s tweak the story. Lisa doesn’t just mentor Emma — she becomes her sponsor. During executive meetings, Lisa mentions Emma’s stellar work on a recent campaign. When a new director role opens up, Lisa directly recommends Emma, saying, “Emma is ready for this. Her work speaks for itself, and she’s already acting at a director level.” The result? Emma gets the opportunity she’s been preparing for, thanks to Lisa’s active sponsorship.

This is the difference. Sponsorship doesn’t just empower — it propels.

What Women Can Do to Secure Sponsors

  • Excel in your current role. Sponsors are more likely to advocate for individuals who consistently deliver results. Make your work undeniable — showcase your skills, take ownership of projects, and exceed expectations.
  • Build strategic relationships. Sponsors are often senior leaders who have the influence to open doors. Look for opportunities to connect with leaders who align with your career aspirations. This doesn’t mean forcing connections — it’s about building genuine relationships based on mutual respect.
  • Be vocal about your goals. A sponsor can’t advocate for you if they don’t know what you want. Be clear and intentional about your career goals, and let potential sponsors see your ambition.
  • Seek out stretch assignments. Volunteer for projects that push you outside your comfort zone. These high-visibility assignments can catch the attention of leaders who may become your sponsors.

What Companies Can Do to Foster Sponsorship

It’s not enough for women to find sponsors on their own. Companies must actively create environments where sponsorship flourishes:

  • Formalize sponsorship programs. Many organizations have mentorship programs, but sponsorship often happens informally. By creating structured sponsorship initiatives, companies can ensure women have access to influential advocates.
  • Hold leaders accountable. Companies should track metrics around promotions, pay equity, and representation in leadership roles. Senior leaders must be held accountable for sponsoring high-potential women and ensuring diverse talent is recognized.
  • Encourage cross-gender sponsorship. Some men hesitate to sponsor women due to fears of appearances or misinterpretation. Companies can address this by normalizing cross-gender sponsorships, providing guidelines, and creating safe, professional structures for these relationships. If business is happening in the male locker room, just think how critical it is to have a sponsor there to advocate for you!
  • Celebrate success stories. When sponsorship leads to tangible results — promotions, increased representation, or impactful projects — celebrate them. Highlighting these stories reinforces the importance of sponsorship and inspires others to get involved.

The Ripple Effect of Sponsorship

When women are sponsored, it’s not just their careers that benefit. The ripple effects are profound. Teams led by women tend to be more collaborative and innovative. Organizations with diverse leadership are more profitable. And perhaps most importantly, when women see other women in positions of power, it inspires the next generation to aim higher.

Sponsorship creates a virtuous cycle. A sponsored woman becomes a leader who, in turn, sponsors others. This is how systemic change happens — not just by breaking barriers, but by building bridges for others to cross.

Sponsorship – the Real Catalyst

If mentorship is about guidance, sponsorship is about advocacy. And in today’s corporate environments, advocacy is what women need most. Sponsorship accelerates careers, challenges biases, and ensures women have a seat — and a voice — at the table.

So, let’s rethink how we support women in the workplace. Mentors are invaluable, but sponsors? They’re the real catalysts for change. If we want to see more women in leadership, we need more sponsors to step up and say, “I believe in her. Let’s make it happen.” That’s the kind of action that moves mountains — and careers.
